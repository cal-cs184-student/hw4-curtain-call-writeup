Part 2: Simulation via Numerical Integration

I enabled simulation of the cloth movement by implementing forces acting on the grid of point masses and springs. This is a three-step process: first, I calculated the total force acting on each point mass, which includes both external acceleration (such as gravity) and spring correction forces, which act in opposite directions on both of a spring's attached masses. I then calculated each point mass's new position for each timestep via Verlet integration, using the formula \( x_{t+dt} = x_t + (1 - d) * (x_t - x_{t-dt}) + a_t * dt^2 \), where \( x_t \) is the mass's current position, \( x_{t-dt} \) is its old position, \( a_t \) is the current total acceleration on the mass from all incident forces, and d is a damping term to account for energy loss due to friction or heat loss. Finally, I constrained the movement of the point masses to ensure that their connecting springs weren't overly deformed, correcting the point masses' positions if they were unpinned and exceeded the threshold distance from each other.

Below are screenshots of scene/pinned2.json with differing values of \( k_s \), the spring constant. At high \( k_s \), the springs connecting each point mass are much more rigid, which results in the cloth appearing less flexible compared to the default value of \( k_s \) = 5000 N/m and not sagging as much. At low \( k_s \), the springs are much looser, which allows the cloth to deform and sag much more in the final resting position by comparison.

Here are screenshots of pinned2.json with different density values. Increasing the density of the cloth increases its mass, and therefore the amount of force acting on each point mass. Consequently, the higher-density cloth appears heavier, with the wrinkles being smaller and sinking deeper. By contrast, the lower-density cloth doesn't wrinkle or sag nearly as much, which makes sense because it has much less force acting on each individual point mass.

The following are screenshots of pinned2.json with different values for the damping constant d. Since damping effectively refers to how much energy is lost during the simulation, high values of d mean that the velocity of the masses is diminished, and therefore the cloth takes longer to get to its final position, falling quite slowly the whole time. With very low damping values, however, the cloth loses less energy to the environment and continues moving past the vertical rest position; at d = 0, the simulation is in near-perpetual and chaotic motion. Part 3: Handling Collisions with Other Objects

I implemented collision handling for spheres and planes according to the suggestions in the spec. For spheres, if the point mass's distance from the sphere's origin was less than or equal to the sphere's radius, I computed the tangent point where the point mass should intersect the surface, then calculated the correction vector that reaches this tangent point from the sphere's last position, and finally set the point mass's current position to be its previous position adjusted by this correction vector and a friction factor. I followed a similar process for determining plane intersections, first identifying if the point mass's position crossed over to the other side of the plane; if so, I calculated the tangent point, then the correction vector to reach a point slightly offset from the tangent point, and finally set the point mass's current position to be its previous one adjusted by the correction vector and friction factor. Lastly, I updated the simulate function call to iterate through all point masses and check if any of them collided with any of the collision objects present in the scene.

Part 4: Handling Self-Collisions

To implement the cloth folding on itself correctly rather than clipping through, I again followed the suggestions outlined in the spec. First, I completed the hash function that maps a given point mass's position in 3D to a single float value that represents the specific box volume that it's contained in; I first calculated the corresponding box volume's values for w, h, and t as suggested in the spec, then created a unique hash value through a combination of exponents. I then populated the map structure so that all point masses in the same box volume are contained in a vector and have the same key, which is their hash value. In self_collide, I used the map to find all other point masses that would potentially collide with a given point mass: if the distance between the given point mass and a candidate is less than \( 2 * thickness \), I calculated the correction vector to apply to the given point mass to bring it to a distance of \( 2 * thickness \) from the candidate mass. After checking all candidates, I then averaged the correction vector by the number of candidates, scaled it by simulation_steps, and updated the point mass's position. Finally, in the simulate function I iterated over all point masses and called self_collide on each to account for the cloth's self-collision overall.

Part 5: Shaders

Shader Programs

Shader programs are isolated programs which run in parallel on a graphics processor and execute a part of the graphics pipeline. Vertex shaders apply transformations to vertices and modify geometric properties such as the vertex normal or position, while fragment shaders process fragments that are created after rasterization and compute their colors based on their geometric properties. Consequently, vertex shaders appear before fragment shaders in the graphics pipeline to operate on vertex data, with fragment shaders directly taking the output of vertex shaders as input. Compiling and linking a vertex shader and a fragment shader produces a shader program; this allows us to create more sophisticated lighting and material effects because we can simulate raised and non-smooth surfaces, map textures to our surfaces, and give our surfaces improved reflective appearances, among other things.

Blinn-Phong Shading

The Blinn-Phong shading model combines ambient lighting, diffuse reflection, and specular highlights to create a very realistic appearance for rendered scenes, represented by the simple equation \( L_{Phong} = L_{ambient} + L_{diffuse} + L_{specular} \). Ambient lighting can be thought of as a base illumination that doesn't depend on anything, not even the surface being rendered; we simply add in constant color to account for the disregarded illumination and soften the shadows which would otherwise be completely black in the scene. The formula for ambient lighing is \( L_{ambient} = k_a * I_a \), where \( k_a \) is the ambient coefficient and \( I_a \) is the ambient color. In diffuse reflection, light scatters uniformly off of the surface in all directions, producing a surface color that is the same in all viewing directions. Diffuse light is given by \( L_{diffuse} = k_d * \frac{I}{r^2} * max(0, n \cdot l) \) where \( k_d \) is the diffuse coefficient, \( I \) is the light intensity, r is the distance between the surface point and the light source, n is the surface normal, and l is the direction vector from the surface point to the light. The final piece of this equation is specular highlights, in which light intensity depends on the view direction and creates more of a glossy, shiny appearance. The equation for specular lighting is \( L_{specular} = k_s * \frac{I}{r^2} * max(0, n \cdot h)^p \), where \( k_s \) is the specular coefficient, h is the vector halfway between the view direction vector and the light direction vector, and p is an "anti-roughness" term that produces a much narrower reflection lobe at high values.

Bump Mapping and Displacement Mapping

Below are screenshots of sphere.json with bump and displacement mapping, using texture_4.png.

Bump mapping creates a mere illusion of raised edges and parts of a surface by modifying the surface's normal vectors and referencing a height map for the texture, whereas displacement mapping modifies both the surface normals and the actual vertices of the surface to reflect the height map. Consequently, bump mapping produces a nicely textured appearance despite the surfaces of the scene remaining smooth, and displacement mapping creates a noticeable change in the geometry of the scene shapes. In the screenshot with displacement mapping, the cloth now has a wave-like ripple and the sphere has consistent divots all over its surface; this makes the texture seem much more ingrained into the scene compared to the rendering with bump mapping.

The screenshots below display bump mapping and displacement mapping on the same scene and texture, but with different resolutions to compare the shaders on different coarseness levels.

Though the results look very similar between the different resolutions, the coarser low-resolution spheres appear to have somewhat flatter reflections, with the specular highlights being not quite as bright. This is more visible for displacement mapping, where the higher-resolution sphere has deeper divots and a less flat-looking highlight.
